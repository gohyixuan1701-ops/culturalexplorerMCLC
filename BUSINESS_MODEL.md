# Cultural Explorer: business model

The Digital Malaysian Chinese Literature Centre (马华文学馆), for judging criterion 03 "Business model & market" (20 marks).

Written 14 November 2026. Every figure is labelled:
- **[source]** = published figure, link in section 7.
- **[proposed]** = our own price or target. It must be tested with real teachers before the pitch.
- **[confirm]** = found in research but not fully verified. Check the original before putting it on a slide.

## 1. The model in one sentence

Schools pay a yearly licence for ready-made lessons and class activities built on a free virtual literature centre, and cultural sponsors pay to add exhibition rooms.

The walk-through stays free. It is the showcase that brings teachers in. The paid part is what saves a teacher preparation time: lesson packs, quizzes, worksheets and class results.

## 2. Business model canvas

| Block | Our answer |
|---|---|
| **Customer segments** | 1. Chinese independent secondary schools (first target). 2. Chinese primary schools (SJKC). 3. Cultural sponsors: foundations, clan associations, publishers. |
| **Users** | Chinese-language teachers and their students. The buyer is the principal or the school board; the user is the teacher. |
| **Value proposition** | For teachers: a 30-minute literature lesson with no preparation. For students: walk in, open the book, compete in a team quiz. For the Centre and sponsors: reach students who will never visit a single room in Johor. |
| **Channels** | Teacher workshops with the Centre and Southern University College, Chinese education bodies (Dong Zong, Jiao Zong), teacher social media groups, a free public link. |
| **Customer relationships** | Free pilot for one term, then a yearly licence. New lesson packs each term keep schools renewing. |
| **Revenue streams** | 1. School licence (yearly). 2. Sponsored exhibition rooms. 3. Custom virtual centres for other museums and associations. 4. Grants for the first year of content. |
| **Key resources** | Permission to use the Centre's texts and covers, the 3D platform, the lesson content, the team. |
| **Key activities** | Writing lessons, securing permissions, onboarding schools, adding rooms. |
| **Key partners** | 马华文学馆 (content and credibility), Southern University College, authors and publishers, sponsors. |
| **Cost structure** | People (content writers, translation, school outreach) are almost all of the cost. Hosting a static web page is close to free at this size. |

## 3. Market size

| Segment | Schools | Students | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chinese primary schools (SJKC) | 1,303 | 463,988 (30 Nov 2025) | [source] 1, 2 |
| Chinese independent secondary schools | 60 + 3 branches | about 82,000 (2025) | school count [source] 3; student total [confirm] in the Dong Zong report |
| **Total** | **1,366** | **about 546,000** | |

Three facts that shape the model:

- **Half of all SJKCs have fewer than 150 pupils.** [source] 2. Small schools cannot pay much, so they need a free or sponsored tier.
- **About one in five SJKC pupils is not Chinese.** Non-Chinese pupils were 18.52% in 2024, and Malay pupils alone were 41,133 (9.01%) in May 2026. [source] 4, 5. This is the evidence for the Chinese / English switch, and for adding Malay.
- **SJKC enrolment fell by 26,526 in 2025, and fewer students take SPM Chinese each year.** [source] 2, 6. This is the problem statement: fewer young people are reaching Chinese literature, and the pitch is that this tool brings it to them.

National-type secondary schools (SMJK) are a further segment. We could not verify their count, so they are left out of the totals.

### Why independent schools first
- They are few (63), so the whole segment can be contacted in one term.
- They are larger: about 1,300 students per school on average (82,000 ÷ 63), against about 356 per SJKC (463,988 ÷ 1,303).
- They teach secondary-level Chinese literature, which matches the Centre's poetry and fiction better than primary school does.

## 4. Pricing

| Tier | Who | Price per school per year | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| Community | Schools with fewer than 150 pupils | Free, paid for by a sponsor | [proposed] |
| Standard | SJKC and other schools | RM 1,200 | [proposed] |
| Large | Independent secondary schools | RM 3,600 | [proposed] |
| Sponsored room | Foundation, association or publisher | RM 5,000 per room, one-off | [proposed] |

How the licence prices were set:

- A sponsor pays RM 60 to RM 80 per student per year to give a student Pandai, a full multi-subject learning platform. [source] 7.
- We cover one topic, not every subject, so our price per student must be a small fraction of that. We used about RM 3 per student per year.
- RM 3 × 1,300 students ≈ RM 3,600 for an average independent school. RM 3 × 356 pupils ≈ RM 1,100, rounded to RM 1,200, for an average SJKC.

This is a starting point, not a tested price. Teacher interviews (section 6) replace it.

### The ceiling, and what it means
If every independent school and every SJKC with 150 or more pupils paid, licence revenue would be about RM 1.0 million a year (63 × RM 3,600 + about 650 × RM 1,200).

That is a small market. Say so in the pitch before a judge does, and answer it with the growth path:

1. **Sponsored rooms** are not limited by the number of schools.
2. **Custom virtual centres** for other museums, clan associations and university collections reuse the same platform. This is the larger business.
3. **More content** (history, festivals, dialect groups) widens the product from one literature centre to a culture platform, which supports a higher licence price.
4. **Grants** can fund year 1 content. MDEC's Digital Content Grant has listed a mini project tier of up to RM 150,000. [confirm] the 2026 terms and whether an education project qualifies. [source] 8.

## 6. What must be validated before the pitch

Criterion 01 "Problem & customer validation" carries the most marks (25) and this model has no customer evidence yet. One afternoon of interviews fixes that and also firms up sections 4 and 5.

Ask three to five Chinese-language teachers or principals:

1. How do you teach Malaysian Chinese literature today, and what is hard about it?
2. What digital teaching material does your school pay for now, and how much?
3. Who decides to buy it, and from which budget?
4. After trying this for five minutes: would you use it in a lesson? What is missing?
5. Would your school pay RM 1,200 (or RM 3,600) a year for it? If not, what price?

Also ask the Centre (马华文学馆) two things: permission to use a few poems or excerpts, and a sentence of support you can quote.

Record the answers as numbers and quotes, for example "4 of 5 teachers said they would use it in class". Put those on the validation slide.
